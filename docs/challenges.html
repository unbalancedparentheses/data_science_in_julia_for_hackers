Data Science in Julia for Hackers

Experiments to try after each chapter

You have followed the example. Can you make a prediction before running the next one? These challenges revisit Chapters 1–5 and 7–13. Chapter 6 and Chapters 14–17 have their own exercises in the chapter. Each sequence starts with a question, changes one part of the experiment, and ends with something to build.

Write down your prediction and its reason first. Keep the original experiment so you can compare it with your change. A useful answer contains a result, an explanation, and one circumstance in which that explanation would fail.

The hints and worked reasoning below are deliberately separate from the tasks. For open projects, the solution describes what a convincing answer needs; there is no single required plot or model. Use each chapter’s environment for code experiments. You can work through the intuition questions without Julia.

Chapter 1 Models and explanations

Predict. Two models predict tomorrow’s temperature equally well. Must they give the same explanation of how the weather works?

Change the experiment. Imagine that one model copies yesterday’s temperature and the other uses a physical simulation. Describe a week in which copying yesterday would be difficult to beat, and a situation that could expose its weakness.

Build. Choose one everyday decision and write a small investigation: the question, observations you could collect, two competing explanations, and an observation that would make you reconsider each explanation.

Hint and worked reasoning

Separate what a model predicts from why it predicts it. During a stable week, copying yesterday may work well. A sudden change in weather can reveal its limitation. The physical model is not guaranteed to win: its measurements or approximations may be poor. Equal predictive performance over one dataset does not establish equal mechanisms. A good investigation identifies evidence that could distinguish the explanations, rather than merely collecting more examples consistent with a favorite story.

Chapter 2 Changing Julia data

Predict. What are a and b after running this code?

a = [1, 2, 3]
b = a
b[1] = 99

Change the experiment. Repeat with b = copy(a). Then compare sort(a) with sort!(a) on a fresh unsorted vector. Print the original vector after each call, not just the returned value.

Build. Write a function that returns a sorted copy and its mean without changing its input. Check it with a singleton vector and repeated values. Choose and explain what your function should do with an empty vector.

Hint and worked reasoning

Assignment gives a and b names for the same mutable vector; both display [99, 2, 3]. copy creates a separate vector for this example. sort returns a sorted copy, while sort! modifies its argument. A possible implementation is summarize(x) = (sorted=sort(x), average=sum(x)/length(x)) for nonempty numeric vectors. Reject an empty vector explicitly, or return missing for its mean. Test the input as well as the returned answer. The exclamation mark is a naming convention, not a language-enforced guarantee about mutation.

Chapter 3 When a rare event looks likely

Predict. A detector catches 90 percent of faulty parts and incorrectly flags 10 percent of good parts. If only one percent of parts are faulty, are most flagged parts faulty?

Change the experiment. Make a table for 10,000 parts, then change the fault rate to 20 percent while keeping both detector rates fixed.

Build. Simulate the detector in Julia. Compare your simulated fraction of faulty flagged parts with the table, and repeat with different sample sizes.

Hint and worked reasoning

Count people or objects before dividing probabilities. At one percent there are 100 faulty parts: 90 are flagged. Of 9,900 good parts, 990 are flagged. Only 90 / (90 + 990), about 8.33 percent of flagged parts, are faulty. At 20 percent, the corresponding fraction is 1800 / (1800 + 800), about 69.23 percent. The detector did not change; the population did. In a simulation, generate each part’s status first and then its flag conditional on that status. Reusing a single unconditional flag probability would erase the relationship we want to investigate. Small simulations fluctuate more; an empty flagged group has an undefined fraction.

Chapter 4 What does a spam filter know

Predict. A message contains only words absent from the training vocabulary. What should the chapter’s classifier return?

Change the experiment. Construct a tiny training corpus with unequal numbers of ham and spam messages. Compare an empty message, an unknown word, and a known word repeated several times. Keep the class counts fixed.

Build. Make a table of the model’s mistakes on held-out messages. Describe which mistake matters more for a personal inbox and for a shop receiving orders. Continue to Chapter 14 to turn those costs into a decision rule.

Hint and worked reasoning

The corrected classifier ignores out-of-vocabulary words. With no known tokens, the posterior equals its class prior, based on message counts. Equal probabilities are appropriate only when the priors are equal. Repeated known tokens contribute repeatedly to the log likelihood in this multinomial model; they need not leave the posterior unchanged. Laplace smoothing gives a known vocabulary word nonzero probability in a class where it was not observed. It does not add every imaginable unseen word to the vocabulary. For the final project, preserve the train/test separation and inspect false positives and false negatives separately; accuracy alone hides the decision’s consequences.

Chapter 5 A coin and a prior

Predict. Starting with a uniform Beta(1, 1) prior, you see eight heads and two tails. Is the posterior mean exactly the observed proportion, 0.8?

Change the experiment. Replace the prior with Beta(20, 20). Then imagine 80 heads and 20 tails. Compare how much the prior changes each answer.

Build. Use the analytic Beta posterior as a reference for the probabilistic program’s samples. Compare the posterior mean and an interval, then increase the number of draws. Explain why matching a mean alone is insufficient.

Hint and worked reasoning

The posterior is Beta(a + heads, b + tails). The first mean is 9/12 = 0.75; with Beta(20, 20), it is 28/50 = 0.56. With 80 heads and 20 tails the two means are 81/102 and 100/140. The same prior has less influence as data accumulate, though it has not disappeared. A sampler can have a plausible mean and still miss a tail or mix poorly. Compare several summaries and inspect the chain. This experiment assumes a fixed success probability and conditionally independent flips; a coin whose behavior changes halfway through asks for another model.

Chapter 7 Football beyond a single prediction

Predict. If we use only the posterior means of attack and defense to simulate matches, have we included uncertainty about the teams’ strengths?

Change the experiment. Compare simulated seasons using fixed posterior mean parameters with seasons using joint posterior draws. Keep each draw’s team parameters together so their posterior relationships are preserved.

Build. Choose an observable feature such as the fraction of scoreless matches or the number of unusually high scores. Compare the observed feature with replicated seasons. Then read Chapter 17 and explain what a simpler model makes easier to diagnose.

Hint and worked reasoning

Even fixed parameters generate random scores, so the first experiment is not deterministic. What it omits is uncertainty about the parameters. Draw a joint parameter sample, simulate a season conditional on it, and repeat to include both sources of uncertainty. Do not promise every chosen statistic will have a wider interval: compare the distributions you actually obtain. A convincing model check picks a feature relevant to the question and looks for systematic disagreement; it does not assume every observed statistic must sit at the center of its predictive distribution.

Chapter 8 Where should we shoot

Predict. One region has 30 made baskets and another has 20. Does that mean the first region gives a higher probability of scoring?

Change the experiment. Give the first region 100 attempts and the second 25. Compare made-shot counts with success rates. Next imagine the second region has only one attempt and one success.

Build. Check a distance-based probability model by grouping held-out shots into distance ranges. Report attempts, successes, and average predicted probability for each group. Identify where the comparison is uncertain.

Hint and worked reasoning

The first two rates are 0.30 and 0.80: attempts change the answer. One success from one attempt gives an empirical rate of 1, but little evidence that future shots are certain to succeed. A map of made shots mixes shot selection with scoring ability. Grouped model checks need denominators and cannot settle individual player effects when players or defensive conditions differ between groups. Define the holdout before choosing model changes; reserve a further assessment if you use these results to improve the model.

Chapter 9 A price with consequences

Predict. If a price maximizes expected revenue, must it maximize expected profit? What if producing each unit costs money?

Change the experiment. For one demand curve Q(p), compare p*Q(p) with (p-c)*Q(p) at several prices and unit costs c. Use a finite positive price grid and inspect its endpoints rather than assuming an interior optimum.

Build. For every posterior draw of the demand curve, calculate profit at each candidate price. Compare the price with highest mean profit and a policy that also considers the probability of a loss.

Hint and worked reasoning

Revenue ignores production costs. Subtracting the same cost per unit does not subtract the same total cost at every price, because quantity changes. Average profits across posterior draws at each fixed price before choosing the expected-profit optimum. Averaging each draw’s preferred price generally does not give the same answer. For a loss-sensitive policy, state the loss threshold and tradeoff; there is no universal risk preference. Finally, Chapter 16 asks whether the estimated demand relationship describes what happens when we actually change the price.

Chapter 10 What is the image classifier looking at

Predict. Could a bee-versus-wasp classifier appear accurate by recognizing photograph backgrounds rather than insects?

Change the experiment. Inspect a small labeled sample. Compare full images with consistently prepared insect crops and background-only views. Write down what each comparison can and cannot tell you before training another model.

Build. Design an evaluation that keeps related photographs together: duplicates, photos of the same specimen, or images from the same source must not cross the relevant train/test boundary. Compare against a majority-class baseline and report mistakes by class.

Hint and worked reasoning

Backgrounds can correlate with labels. A random file split may preserve those correlations or even separate near-duplicates, inflating apparent performance. A background-only model that predicts labels is evidence of a shortcut in the dataset. Poor performance after cropping alone is not proof of a shortcut: cropping also changes resolution and image composition. A strong answer documents the split unit and preprocessing, keeps the final test untouched, and explains which future population that test represents. Start by auditing the images; another expensive training run is not the first required step.

Chapter 11 An ecosystem with new players

Predict. In the chapter’s basic predator–prey equations, what happens to the prey derivative if the prey population is exactly zero? What about predators when no prey remain?

Change the experiment. Start with small positive populations and increase the player-induced mortality. Compare trajectories, then shorten the solver’s step or tighten its tolerances to distinguish numerical behavior from the equations’ behavior.

Build. Propose one missing mechanism—migration, a protected reserve, or limited resources—and write the term you would add. Explain its units, sign, and behavior at zero population before fitting anything.

Hint and worked reasoning

In the basic model, population changes are proportional to the population: zero prey stays zero, and predators without prey decay under positive mortality. Numerical approximations can produce undesirable values, which is why solver checks matter. An immigration term can permit recovery from zero; a birth term proportional to the absent population cannot. A carrying-capacity term changes growth at large populations. The best extension is the one whose mechanism and observable consequences you can explain, not the one with the largest number of parameters.

Chapter 12 Can we recover the missing rule

Predict. If two learned differential equations reproduce one observed trajectory, must they agree for a different initial population?

Change the experiment. Compare their predictions from several initial conditions outside the fitting trajectory. Then add observation noise and check whether your conclusion changes.

Build. For a candidate recovered equation, make a short scientific report: training conditions, held-out initial conditions, units of each term, prediction errors, and behavior near the edge of the observed region.

Hint and worked reasoning

One trajectory explores only part of the state space. Different vector fields can agree there and disagree elsewhere. A low fitting error alone therefore does not identify the governing law. Sparse expressions can improve interpretability, but the chosen basis and threshold influence what can be recovered. Use the chapter’s known synthetic mechanism to check equation recovery, then separate that advantage from what would be knowable with real data. Do not tune repeatedly on the same held-out initial conditions and continue to call them an independent final test.

Chapter 13 A forecast that reacts too slowly

Predict. A series stays at 10 and then jumps to 20. Which simple exponential smoother reacts faster: alpha 0.2 or alpha 0.8?

Change the experiment. With the previous level initialized to 10, compute the first two level updates after observing 20 twice. Then add random noise around an unchanged level and compare how jumpy the two smoothers become.

Build. Evaluate one-step forecasts with a rolling origin: predict the next observation using only the past, record its error, and then update. Compare with predicting the most recent observation. Keep a final time window separate from the one used to choose alpha.

Hint and worked reasoning

Using level = alpha*observation + (1-alpha)*previous_level, alpha 0.2 gives 12 then 13.6; alpha 0.8 gives 18 then 19.6. Faster adaptation also responds more strongly to noise. The level after seeing an observation is available for the next forecast; using it to predict the observation just consumed leaks information. Randomly shuffling time points destroys the forecasting question. A complete answer also asks whether the simple level-only model can represent the trend or seasonality present in the series.
